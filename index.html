01

3 pressures now hit India at the same time: dear money, dear oil and a US tariff law

Why Indian shares have fallen for 7 weeks in a row, and how the 3 pressures feed each other.

In short
  • Borrowing money in the US now costs more than at any time since 2007.
  • Oil is still expensive because the Strait of Hormuz is only partly open.
  • A new US law lets Washington put tariffs of up to 100% on countries that buy Russian oil, and India is one of the biggest buyers.

The price of money went up again

A yield is what a lender demands each year to lend money for a set time. The yield on the US government's benchmark bond reached 5.21%, and the Federal Reserve, the US central bank, raised its rate in September for the first time in about 3 years. When the safest borrower in the world pays over 5%, every other borrower pays more too. That includes Indian companies and the Indian government.

Foreign funds sold on 4 of the last 5 trading days, and Indian funds bought more than they sold. This is the pattern that has held all year. Local savings, mostly through monthly mutual fund plans, are the only reason the market has not fallen much further.

  • Futures — on 25 September, 89% of foreign investors' positions in index futures were bets on a fall (short positions).
  • Trading volume — daily turnover in futures and options fell to a 34-month low of ₹1.69 lakh crore in September.
  • Big names — Infosys fell for 8 days in a row, its longest losing run since April 2024. HDFC Bank took back the number 2 spot among India's most valuable companies, worth about $118 billion.

India has done worse than almost every neighbour since Covid

A rolling return is the average yearly gain of an index over the years just finished. On a 5-year view, India's returns have sunk back to where they were in 2020.

  • Nifty 50 — about 9% a year over 5 years.
  • Nifty 500 — about 8% a year, against a long-run average of 12%.
  • Against Pakistan — measured in rupees, Indian shares are doing worse against Pakistani shares than at any time since at least 2013.

The gap is not only about India. Korea's market is full of chip makers, which have gained from the AI spending boom, and India has few such companies. So money chasing AI has gone to markets like Seoul, not Mumbai.

Oil is still the cost that will not go away

The war between the US and Iran is now in its 8th month. Oil moving through the Strait of Hormuz, the narrow sea lane between Iran and Oman, has recovered to about two-thirds of the pre-war level. Iran keeps attacking ships, and President Trump rejected Iran's plan to reopen the strait. The full story of the oil market is in section 04.

A US law now names the buyers of Russian oil

US Congress passed, and the President signed on 18 September, the Sanctioning Russia and Iran Act (called SRIA). It lets the US put tariffs of up to 100% on goods from the 5 biggest buyers of Russian crude oil and gas. The law does not name India, but India is one of the largest buyers. The first actions are due within 30 days, by 18 October.

  • Russia itself — up to 500% tariffs on Russian goods.
  • Waiver — the President can waive any of it by saying it serves the US national interest.
  • Diplomacy — a US Congress group led by Brian Mast, who chairs the House Foreign Affairs Committee, met Foreign Secretary Vikram Misri in Delhi and discussed the law.
  • The mood in Delhi — Navtej Sarna, India's former ambassador to the US, wrote that India should "call the bully's bluff", even if standing up means economic setbacks.

So the risk for Indian exporters is no longer a rumour. It has a date. Oil refiners that buy Russian crude, and exporters of goods to the US, face the biggest danger if the waiver is not used.

How the 3 pressures connect

Each pressure makes the others worse. Dear oil keeps inflation high, which keeps US rates high. High US rates pull foreign money out of India. A weaker flow of foreign money makes it harder for India to pay for dear oil. The SRIA law could take away the cheaper Russian barrels that have eased the oil bill so far.

The companies that do best are the ones selling to buyers who still have cash, such as defence, space, grid batteries and foreign oil and gas projects. Section 06 names them.

Cooking gas: refiners are squeezing out more at home

LPG is the bottled gas used for cooking. About 90% of India's LPG imports used to pass through Hormuz, so the war hit kitchens directly. State refiners raised LPG output to 44,000 tonnes a day in September, about 20% more than in August.

  • New routes — India now also buys from the US and Africa. The UAE is still the biggest supplier; it uses a pipeline to the port of Fujairah that avoids Hormuz, plus small ships.
  • October — ADNOC, the UAE's state oil company, has promised its contracted volumes for October.
  • Demand — home demand rises in the festival season, while some factories are using less gas because it is dear.

So the gap is being filled from home refineries and new sellers. The fix works for now, but it depends on the UAE's pipeline route staying safe, which means 1 more attack on Fujairah would put kitchens back at risk.

Urea: a ₹2 trillion plan to stop importing

Urea is the most common nitrogen fertiliser. India is the world's largest urea importer and buys about a quarter of what it needs from abroad. The government's new urea policy, called NIPU-2026, aims to close that gap with new plants.

  • Demand — about 40 million tonnes a year, growing about 5% a year.
  • Target — 10 million tonnes of new capacity within about 3 years of approval.

But urea plants run on natural gas, which also comes by sea. So new plants cut the fertiliser import bill but add to the gas import bill, unless India signs long-term gas deals.

Batteries: China will not share the recipe

LFP (lithium iron phosphate) is the cheaper, safer battery type used in most grid storage and many electric cars. JSW Group has put its planned 50 GWh LFP battery plant on hold because it could not find a Chinese technology partner. Other Indian firms, including Reliance Industries, have struggled for the same reason.

A GWh (gigawatt-hour) measures how much energy a battery holds; 50 GWh is a very large plant. The point being made across the material is that India must build its own battery technology or pay royalties forever. China's lead in rare earths shows how long such a lead lasts: its chemists first separated 2 key rare earth metals in 1974.

Ethanol: the opposite problem, too much capacity

Ethanol is alcohol made from sugarcane or grain and mixed into petrol. The government promised to buy it at a fixed price to reach 20% blending (E20). Distillers rushed in, and now about 7 billion litres of capacity has no buyer.

  • Use of plants — distilleries run at about 60% of capacity, and are expected to stay at 65% to 75% for 3 years.
  • No escape through exports — only second-generation ethanol (made from farm waste) may be exported. The E20 limit stays until 31 October 2026, and plans for E25 and E30 are paused after complaints from car owners.
  • A new outlet — Praj Industries expects its first order for bio-isobutanol, a fuel that can be mixed into diesel, this quarter. It says a 2% diesel mandate would be a project opportunity worth over ₹3,000 crore.

This is a classic capital cycle: money rushes to where returns look guaranteed, and the rush itself destroys the guarantee. Before buying a theme, count the capacity being built. The same test now applies to battery storage, solar cells and data centres in section 05 and section 06.

03

Money and rates: lenders everywhere want more, and India is borrowing less to cope

US bond yields, the credit market, gold, and how India's government and banks are adjusting.

In short
  • Every kind of US lending now pays more than it has for years.
  • India's government cut how much it will borrow this year by ₹1.2 lakh crore to calm its own bond market.
  • Indian bank deposits are growing at the fastest pace since 2011, faster than loans.

Even the biggest companies feel it. Meta's longest bond has fallen to about $89 per $100, so it now yields about 7.2%, though Meta has a very strong credit rating. So investors are separating a company's share price from its debt: Meta's shares rose in the month while its bond fell.

  • Rate swings — the MOVE index, which measures how much bond prices jump around, rose to about 96, above its long average of 80. The gap between company and government yields has barely moved, so credit markets have not yet priced in the swings.
  • The basis trade — hedge funds borrow up to 50 times their money to profit from tiny price gaps between government bonds and bond futures. The trade is about $900 billion. It blew up once, in March 2020, when rates swung hard.
  • Missing buyer — East Asia runs a trade surplus of about $1.7 trillion a year, but its purchases of long-term US bonds are close to zero. The US needs that money to fund its deficit.
  • Weak demand — a recent 5-year US bond auction drew the weakest demand since 2018.

So the bond market is being asked to take more debt with fewer natural buyers. That is why yields keep rising even when growth looks fine.

Foreign money is still piling into US shares

While bonds struggle, US shares keep drawing foreign money. Foreign investors put a record $426 billion into US stocks in the second quarter, far above the old record of $299 billion in 2022.

  • Holdings — foreigners held $24.32 trillion of US shares in July, up $2.22 trillion this year.
  • Share of wallet — US shares are now a record 60% of foreigners' US investments, about 6 points above the peak of the 2000 dot-com bubble.
  • Where it goes — the 10 biggest AI-linked companies are 41% of the US market's value; section 08 explains why that number matters.

The US shopper is split into 2 groups

US households are saving little, and more card debt is late, but the stress sits mostly with weaker borrowers.

  • Savings — households save about 3% of income, near a 20-year low, against 7% to 8% before the pandemic.
  • Late card debt — 12.8% of card balances are 90 days late, against 7.6% in late 2022.
  • New late payments — the flow has been flat since 2024.

The richest 10% of US households now do almost half of all spending. So a stock market fall would hit US spending harder than before, because the rich spend from their share gains. A key inflation reading (the PCE index) is due on 30 September; a change in how it is measured will lower the yearly figure by about 0.2 points.

India: borrow less, and borrow for longer

India's own long bond yield has risen for 6 weeks in a row to 7.12%. The government answered by borrowing less and for longer.

  • Full year — borrowing cut to ₹15.99 lakh crore, against ₹17.2 lakh crore in the budget.
  • Deficit — the target stays at 4.3% of GDP.

Money is moving out of savings accounts, which pay little, into fixed deposits, which pay more. That raises banks' cost of money. So even with strong deposit growth, bank profit margins are under pressure, which fits with bank shares being cut in some active funds (section 08).

More of every salary goes into provident fund

The Cabinet raised the wage ceiling of the EPFO (Employees' Provident Fund Organisation), the level of pay on which provident fund is compulsory.

  • Ceiling — from ₹15,000 to ₹25,000 a month, from 17 September.
  • Reach — about 51 lakh more workers come under compulsory coverage.
  • Pay slip — for someone earning ₹25,000, the employee's share rises from ₹1,800 to ₹3,000 a month.

So take-home pay falls a little, and long-term savings rise. It also means more money flows into government bonds, because the EPFO invests mostly in them.

2 stories about the same strait

Iran's Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi said Iran will reopen the strait on day 6 of a 7-day plan, and resume talks on day 7, if the US meets conditions first. Those include lifting naval blockades and freeing frozen Iranian money. President Trump told Axios the Iranians "overplayed their hand" but that he expects more talks.

  • The US view — Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent said "the Straits are open", that only 15 million barrels of Iranian oil are left at sea, and that Iran will make its last deliveries to China within 2 weeks.
  • Iran's view — Mohammad Bagher Ghalibaf, speaker of Iran's parliament, said the market prices a $35 premium over pre-war, or $50 using physical cargo prices, so Iran clearly still controls the strait.
  • China — Bessent said China has "substantially reduced" its help to Iran.
  • The UN — US envoy Mike Waltz said 146 countries, a UN record, have condemned Iran's attacks on shipping.

Both views can be true. Flows are up because Saudi Arabia found ways round, and prices stay high because every tanker still carries war risk. So Brent above $100 is a price for risk, not for a shortage of oil. The 2 sides' cases are set out in section 10.

India builds its own safety nets at sea

To cut its dependence on foreign marine insurers during the war, India set up its own insurance pool, the Bharat Maritime Insurance Pool, launched on 12 May.

  • Policies — over 3,000 cargo war-risk policies, 92 hull war-risk policies and 3 P&I policies (Protection and Indemnity, which covers a ship owner's legal liabilities) by 7 September.
  • Backing — underwriting capacity of ₹13,906.5 crore (about $1.5 billion), with a government guarantee of ₹12,980 crore. Members include New India Assurance and the Shipping Corporation of India.
  • Why — India pays $45 million to $60 million a year in P&I premiums to foreign insurers.
  • Green fuel — Deendayal Port Authority at Kandla and Assam Petro-Chemicals began building a ₹23 billion ($240 million) e-methanol plant making 150 tonnes a day; e-methanol is a ship fuel made from green hydrogen and captured carbon.
  • Clean ports — Adani Ports and SEZ's Dhamra Port now runs fully on renewable power, over 108 GWh a year, 25% to 30% of it from the Khavda solar and wind park. It handled 48.8 million tonnes in FY26.

Gas: Asia keeps building despite the shock

Southeast Asia has 100 GW of gas power plants and 70 GW of gas import terminals under construction, even though LNG (liquefied natural gas, shipped cold as a liquid) became scarce and dear in the war. The bet rests on 3 hopes: steady supply, affordable prices and local gas fields as backup. So the region is doubling down on the fuel that just failed it, because there is no quick alternative.

PB Fintech, which runs Policybazaar, held an investor call afterwards. It gave the clearest numbers on what the change means.

  • Agent pay — an agent who earns about ₹15,000 on a new health policy today could earn ₹3,500 to ₹3,750.
  • General insurance — selling income could fall to one-third to 40% of today's level. The near-term hit to PB Fintech's core revenue could be about 30%.
  • Offset — lower commissions could lower premiums. Assuming each 1% fall in price lifts demand 1%, higher volumes could recover 15% to 20% of the hit.
  • Hiring — it added about 6,000 staff in the first half; under the new rules it would have needed about 2,000.
  • New income — it may charge for services it now gives free: claims help, hospital networks and garages. It may also move closer to making insurance products itself.

The deeper point came from those who had already sold: over recent years industry commissions grew about 22% a year while premiums grew about 10%. That gap could not last. A similar reform over a decade ago took life insurers years to recover from. So the whole distribution chain, from banks to online brokers, is being repriced at once.

Grid batteries: from pilot to industry in a year

BESS (battery energy storage systems) store solar power by day and release it at night. India installed 8.2 GWh of BESS in the first half of 2026, 83 times more than a year earlier.

  • Standalone — 83% of installations are batteries on their own, not attached to a solar farm, so grid operators can use them freely.
  • Rajasthan — it has 36% of India's batteries, because its big desert solar parks keep hitting grid limits.
  • Pipeline — tenders jumped 86% to 28 GW in the half year.

This links straight to the battery problem in section 02: demand for grid batteries is surging just as the main battery technology is locked up in China. So Indian battery makers that do not depend on Chinese partners, such as HBL Engineering with its special lead batteries (section 06), sit in the gap.

Space and deep tech: patient money from the state

  • Agnikul Cosmos — the Technology Development Board will give ₹200 crore as optionally convertible debentures (loans that can turn into shares) to build Agnibaan, a fully reusable rocket.
  • Space economy — India has over 400 space startups that have raised nearly $900 million. The sector is projected to grow from about $8.4 billion now to $44 billion by 2033.
  • Hand-off — private firms such as Skyroot Aerospace, Pixxel, Dhruva Space, GalaxEye and Bellatrix Aerospace are taking routine launches and satellites, so ISRO can focus on human spaceflight (Gaganyaan), a Moon sample return and a space station.
  • IIT Madras fund — the Unicorn Frontier Fund, run with Unicorn India Ventures, raised ₹450 crore in its first close, has invested ₹55 crore in 4 startups, and targets ₹1,000 crore by December.

The risk named in the material: if private firms only do "cheap rides and small satellites", ISRO could shrink without the hard skills moving to the private side. So the next 3 years depend on whether engines, materials and guidance systems move into private hands too.

Defence: more money, more of it spent at home

Defence Minister Rajnath Singh said 75% of the capital budget, ₹1.39 lakh crore, now goes to Indian companies, and all 16 defence public-sector companies make profits. He said India is watching the development of sixth-generation fighter aircraft closely.

  • Startups — the iDEX scheme has worked with 676 startups and signed 551 contracts.
  • Navy — it is developing Indian-made ship satellite terminals, called Rukmani, to replace imports, using the GSAT-7R satellite.
  • Research — DRDO began building an underwater surveillance test facility in Kochi.
  • Pakistan — its navy test-fired a longer-range anti-ship missile from its new submarine PNS Hangor.

Other rules and state spending

  • Private investment — the Reserve Bank of India says companies plan ₹3.2 lakh crore of capital spending in FY27 on projects already in the pipeline, 27% more than a year earlier. Power alone is 39.6% of sanctioned project costs.
  • Bank strike — a 3-day strike by public-sector bank staff from 28 September was called off late on 27 September after talks with the banks' association, which agreed to study more Saturday holidays.
  • Sunday duty — 2 Union Bank of India staff died in road accidents while travelling for work on the Sunday banks were told to open before the planned strike.
